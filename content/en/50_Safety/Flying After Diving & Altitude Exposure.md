# Flying After Diving & Altitude Exposure

[[en/70_Resources/Sidemount Gear Travel Packing & Airline Baggage Guide|Sidemount Gear Travel Packing & Airline Baggage Guide]] covers whether your **luggage** can board the aircraft; this note covers when **your body** can. Ascending to a lower-pressure environment after diving — flying, crossing a mountain pass, even driving home over an elevated route — allows residual nitrogen to keep expanding. It is an independent trigger for decompression sickness (DCS), regardless of how correctly the dive itself was executed.

Taiwan's dive geography makes this concrete: Green Island and Orchid Island are served by **unpressurised** commuter aircraft, and the Central Mountain Range separates the east and west coasts with road passes above 3,000 m.

> ⚠️ **This note collects safety numbers; it replaces neither training nor medical judgement.** Anyone with suspected DCS symptoms **must not fly** and should contact DAN or a diving physician first (see [[en/50_Safety/DCS Symptom Recognition & Oxygen First Aid|DCS Symptom Recognition & Oxygen First Aid]] and [[en/70_Resources/Diving Insurance & Emergency Evacuation Planning|Diving Insurance & Emergency Evacuation Planning]]).

---

## 🫧 1. Why Ascending to Altitude Causes Illness

After a dive, tissues still hold nitrogen above surface equilibrium; clearing it takes hours to over a dozen hours. If ambient pressure then **drops further**:

*   Residual nitrogen supersaturation is amplified, and bubble nuclei that would have stayed harmless can grow into symptomatic bubbles.
*   The mechanism is identical to ascending too fast (see [[en/50_Safety/Ascent Rate & Safety Stop Standards|Ascent Rate & Safety Stop Standards]]); only the scale changes, from "1 bar lost in minutes" to "0.2–0.3 bar lost over hours".
*   **This is about pressure ratio, not absolute altitude** — which is why a 600 m road and a 2,400 m cabin sit on the same risk spectrum, differing only in degree.

---

## ✈️ 2. Flying After Diving: The DAN/UHMS Consensus Intervals

The most widely adopted standard comes from the **2002 Flying After Diving workshop held jointly by DAN and UHMS** [1][2]:

| Dive profile | Minimum preflight surface interval |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Single no-decompression dive** | **12 hours** |
| **Multiple dives per day, or multiple days of diving** | **18 hours** |
| **Dives requiring decompression stops** | **Substantially longer than 18 hours** (evidence is thin; no firm consensus value) |

*   **Applicability**: cabin altitudes of **610–2,438 m (2,000–8,000 ft)**, air diving, and divers with **no DCS symptoms** [1][2].
*   **Experimental basis**: for single no-stop dives to 18 m or deeper, no DCS cases occurred with surface intervals of 11 hours or longer; for repetitive no-stop dives, cases occurred at intervals shorter than 17 hours [2]. The consensus values round up from there with added margin.
*   **The limits of the consensus** (understand these): trials were run in a **dry hyperbaric chamber with resting volunteers** [1]. Real diving adds immersion, exercise and cooling, all of which worsen gas loading and bubble behaviour. The guidance "should reduce the DCS risk… but does not guarantee avoidance of DCS" [1].

> 📌 **Your computer's no-fly countdown is not this guideline.** Shearwater states that they **deliberately do not** provide a Time to Fly countdown: "We have chosen not to reduce these recommendations to a calculation", because the guidance depends on dive type, decompression obligation and the presence of symptoms — none of which a computer can determine — so they ask divers to apply the DAN guidance themselves [3]. Where a countdown exists, it is a **theoretical tissue half-saturation estimate**, not the FAD consensus value; take whichever is **longer**.

## 🚗 3. Crossing Mountains by Road: Taiwan's Overlooked Half

The physiology is identical to flying; almost nobody thinks of driving as altitude exposure. DAN advises applying the same 12/18-hour intervals before ascending above **610 m (2,000 ft)** [4].

Taiwan's road elevations:

| Route | Highest point | Relative to the 610 m threshold |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Highway 14A · Wuling** | **3,275 m** (km 31.5; Taiwan's highest road point) [5] | Over 5× |
| **Highway 8 · Dayuling** (highest point of the main Central Cross-Island Highway) | approx. **2,565 m** (road surface 2,564.67 m) [6] | Over 4× |

*   **Typical trap**: diving Hualien or the Northeast Coast, then driving the Central Cross-Island Highway (Route 8) home to Taichung the same day; or diving Kenting and going up Hehuanshan for sunrise the next morning. Either can put you above 2,500 m well short of a 12-hour interval.
*   **Alternatives usually exist**: east-coast routes via the Suhua or South-Link corridors (Route 9) stay near sea level. Schedule the high-mountain leg **before** the diving, or allow a full interval.
*   High-speed rail and the western freeway corridor are at negligible elevation and are not a concern.

## 🛩️ 4. Unpressurised Aircraft: Cabin Altitude Equals Flight Altitude

Flights from Taitung Fengnian Airport to **Green Island and Orchid Island** are operated by Daily Air using the **DHC-6-400 Twin Otter**, a 19-seat aircraft with an **unpressurised cabin** [7].

*   Pressurised airliners hold the cabin at roughly 1,800–2,400 m equivalent altitude, which is a form of protection. **An unpressurised aircraft offers none — cabin altitude is the actual flight altitude.**
*   The legs are short and flown low, so real exposure usually falls within or below the band the DAN guidance addresses. But **a 30-minute flight does not make the risk disappear**; count the interval anyway.
*   **Practical advice**: leave the last day of a Green Island / Orchid Island trip dive-free, or return by ferry (transport modes per island are covered in [[en/70_Resources/Taiwan Sidemount Dive Sites Suitability Guide|Taiwan Sidemount Dive Sites Suitability Guide]]).

## ⛰️ 5. The Reverse Case: Diving At Altitude

Not diving then going up, but **entering the water up there** — rare in Taiwan, common on overseas mountain-lake trips.

*   **Threshold**: elevations of **300 m (about 1,000 ft)** and above count as altitude diving and require altitude procedures [4][8]. Standard decompression tables and computer defaults are calibrated at sea level.
*   **Why**: surface pressure is lower (about 0.93 atm at 2,000 ft), so the same water depth represents a larger pressure ratio, degrading both off-gassing and ascent margin [4].
*   **Dive computers**: units with a barometric sensor sample local pressure at power-on; those without must have the **altitude mode set manually**, and the fresh/salt water setting confirmed [4].
*   **Wait after arriving**: the US Navy recommends waiting **12 hours** after arriving at altitude before the first dive, letting the body equilibrate to the new ambient pressure [8].
*   **Sidemount specifics**: fresh water provides roughly 2–3% less buoyancy than salt water, so weighting needs a fresh surface check (see [[en/20_Equipment/Steel vs Aluminum Buoyancy Management|Steel vs Aluminum Buoyancy Management]] and [[en/30_Skills/In-Water Buoyancy & Horizontal Trim|In-Water Buoyancy & Horizontal Trim]]). The 10 m = 1 bar approximation behind the MOD formula does not hold at altitude and should be made conservative (see [[en/40_Gas_Management/Nitrox Basics & MOD Calculation|Nitrox Basics & MOD Calculation]]).

---

> 📋 **Assumptions & Limitations**
> *   12/18 hours are **minimum** recommendations, not targets — longer intervals lower risk further [1]. If you have slack, use it.
> *   The consensus is based on **air diving** with dry, resting subjects; nitrox, trimix, CCR and high-workload profiles are not covered and warrant more conservatism.
> *   Individual variation is large: some people show high-grade bubbles during flight even after a 24-hour interval [2]. Poor condition, dehydration, fatigue and PFO all raise risk (PFO is covered in [[en/60_Advanced/Decompression Theory Models & Physiology|Decompression Theory Models & Physiology]]).
> *   Conservative gradient factors ([[en/40_Gas_Management/Decompression Planning Software & Gradient Factors|Decompression Planning Software & Gradient Factors]]) reduce residual loading at take-off but **cannot be traded for a shorter surface interval**.
> *   These are preventive intervals for **asymptomatic divers**. Management of an actual case is a medical matter — see [[en/50_Safety/DCS Symptom Recognition & Oxygen First Aid|DCS Symptom Recognition & Oxygen First Aid]].
